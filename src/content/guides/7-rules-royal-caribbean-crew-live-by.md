## 1. Friendliness has a job description

The guest policy says the quiet part in a polite sentence. "Please do not misinterpret their friendliness."

Crew are prohibited from physical relationships with guests. They are not permitted to socialize with guests beyond their professional duties. They are not permitted in a guest stateroom except to do shipboard work. The rule runs the other way too. Guests are prohibited from physical relationships with crew. Guests are not permitted in restricted or crew areas, including crew staterooms and corridors.

A long chat while someone makes your drink sits inside the job. An invitation to the balcony after the shift is a different event. Under the code, a breach of company policy can end in discipline, up to termination.

The code points to a separate document, the Harassment, Guest Interaction, and Crime Policy. A search on 8 Nov 2026 did not turn up the text. The code names it.

The same guest policy draws a line about personal care, and it is easy to over-read. Crew are not required to help with personal tasks or personal hygiene. The examples are eating, dressing, and toileting. A guest with a disability is not required to bring another person. If you plan to travel alone, and there is a question about whether you can handle those needs without being a danger to yourself or others, you discuss it with the Royal Caribbean Group Access Department before you book. The policy says that conversation lets the company assess fitness to travel. A guest found unfit may be denied boarding, or removed at the next port, without compensation or a refund.

The policy is a condition of boarding and of staying on board, including at the private destinations. A breach can mean denial of boarding or removal, without compensation or a refund. The travel party can be removed as well. A guest who is removed pays for their own room and their own way home, and handles their own documents for re-entry. The captain keeps overriding authority for the safety and security of the ship.

On your cruise, enjoy the person who remembers your coffee. Leave the friendship where they are working. Stay out of crew corridors. If you need help with eating, dressing, or toileting, and you are traveling alone, call the access department before you pay a deposit.

## 2. A slow gangway stays slow

Every port brings customs, immigration, and port officials. There is a folk name for a small payment that hurries routine government work. The code bans it, and it uses the name. Facilitation or grease payments are prohibited.

Employees may not make those payments themselves or through someone else. Cash counts. So does a payment in kind. The code's examples are liquor and cigarettes. The banned purpose is expediting routine government action, such as processing visas, clearing customs, or scheduling inspections.

The code's definition of a bribe is wider than an envelope. Money, gifts, hospitality, favors, and political or charitable contributions count when they are used to influence someone improperly.

Employees are also told to watch outside agents who deal with officials. Red flags in the code include an agent who offers to get around red tape, a request for cash or for payment through an unknown third party, and an agent who will not provide detailed invoices. The code tells employees to consult the company's Anti-Bribery Policy for the rest. The sentences above are the ones printed in the code itself.

For you, a gangway that stays closed is frustrating. The code bars crew from handing over a bottle, a carton, or cash to move your departure up. Ask Guest Services what the hold is.

## 3. The person who knows your drink is not the spokesperson

You will see a clip of a pool deck argument, or a rumor about a new fee, and the crew stay out of the replies. The code explains the silence.

Only company spokespersons who are expressly authorized may speak for the company in public, online, or on social media. If a reporter, another third party, or a government office asks a crew member for information, the code says do not answer. Forward the request to Corporate Communications.

The code even runs a sample case. An employee sees a rumor online, knows it is false, and wants to correct it. The answer is no, unless that employee has been authorized to speak for the company. Tell Corporate Communications, and let them decide.

Personal accounts are still allowed, with conditions. The code says to use good judgment, follow company values, and keep confidential information off the page. Every email, text, and chat is supposed to be written as if it could become public.

A group [Social Media Policy dated 28 Oct 2024](https://my.silversea.com/fls/otherdocs/SocialMediaPolicy.pdf) adds the working detail. Only authorized employees may speak for the company. Someone who thinks an official reply is needed takes it to Corporate Communications and does not post one on their own. A post that touches the business needs a disclaimer that the person is speaking for themself. Posts about non-public operations, including back-of-house spaces on ships, about company policies, or about competitors, also need written approval from Corporate Communications. The policy tells employees not to post rumors or speculation about the company, other employees, or guests.

The 2026 code adds an information rule that was built for this decade. Employees may not paste confidential company information into public generative AI tools. Business use stays on AI systems the company has authorized.

The code also prints a four-question test for a hard call. Is the action legal? Do the code and company policy permit it? Does it reflect the company's values? Would it reflect favorably on the employee and the company if it became public? If any answer is no, the code says do not do it.

On your cruise, let a bartender be a bartender. A question about a viral clip, a fee rumor, or a news story belongs with the company's public statements, or with reporting that cites them. A crew member's personal account is their own post, and the policy tells them to label it that way.

## 4. Staying quiet is its own breach

The code calls speaking up an obligation. That covers something the employee sees, and something they see at a supplier or another business partner.

The routes are a supervisor or manager, Human Resources, the Global Compliance and Ethics Office, Audit and Advisory Services, and the Legal Department. An employee can also email the audit committee of the board at auditcommittee@rccl.com.

There is a hotline called AWARE. An independent company runs it. It is open 24 hours a day, every day of the year, in the employee's preferred language. The employee may report without giving a name where local law permits. The code encourages people to identify themselves, because a name makes a response easier.

The dialing instructions are specific, and the ship line is easy to mis-hear. From the ship, the code says dial `**88`. From the United States, U.S. territories, and Canada, it says dial 1-888-81-AWARE, which is 1-888-812-9273. From other countries, the employee uses [rclaware.ethicspoint.com](https://rclaware.ethicspoint.com) to get the local number, or places a collect call to 1-678-250-7527 if the country is not listed. Online reports use the same site.

The code's examples of what belongs on that line include corruption, fraud, or theft, safety and environmental concerns, violations of law, and retaliation against someone who reported in good faith. A sample case about a news story on a supplier's labor practices says to report it so the company can investigate. A sample about garbage in the ocean near the ship says the same, even if the employee does not know where the garbage came from.

Retaliation for a good-faith report is prohibited. A report the employee knows is false is a violation too.

Guests have their own version, and it is a boarding condition. You report your own injury, someone else's injury, and any unsafe or possibly illegal behavior, right away, to the ship's security staff or other ship management. The policy lists the emergency number in the Directory of Services, the Guest Services desk, or security and management wherever you find them. The guest path is that list. The `**88` line is the instruction the code gives employees.

If you see something unsafe, tell security or Guest Services while the ship can still do something with it. The policy says a delay can cost the ship evidence, and can slow a call to the authorities.

## 5. The job has a price list, and a fee is not on it

Rule 5 comes from the convention, and the careers pages show how the company describes it.

Under the convention, a seafarer does not bear fees or other charges for recruitment or placement, or for being provided with employment. The exceptions are narrow. The seafarer can bear the cost of a national statutory medical certificate, the national seafarer's book, and a passport or similar personal travel document. The cost of visas is for the shipowner. The ILO FAQ adds a limit on that sentence. The convention does not say whether "the cost of visas" includes travel to another country to obtain the visa. A collective agreement or an employment agreement can speak to the document costs too.

The [careers FAQ](https://careers.royalcaribbeangroup.com/ship/) says the company never asks candidates to pay fees to apply, to interview, or to get hired. It warns about scams that pose as cruise lines or hiring agencies. Only hiring partners listed on the site are authorized to recruit.

The [hiring partners page](https://careers.royalcaribbeangroup.com/hiring-partners-02/) says the company pays those partners and does not allow them to collect administrative fees, agency fees, or processing fees. It also lists costs of joining that sit outside that ban: a passport, seaman's books, national identification documents, medicals, airline tickets, and certifications or licenses the role requires. There is no fee for the interview, the position, or the ship assignment. Once the person joins the ship, the cost of travel visas is reimbursed. Service charges and associated fees for obtaining the visa are not reimbursed. That reimbursement line is the company's published practice. It is narrower than a slogan that every visa charge is covered.

A few other hiring lines are on the same FAQ, and they are worth keeping straight.

Applicants must be at least 18. Beverage serving positions require 21. English is the official language on board, and employees must be able to read, write, and speak it. Some roles ask for another language as well. A valid passport is required. So is a seaman's book, a criminal background check, and the ability to obtain the visas an itinerary needs. The documentation list also includes flu vaccines and negative COVID-19 tests. Those are the words on the page read on 8 Nov 2026.

The C1-D visa gets mis-summarized a lot. The careers page says it is a non-immigrant visa that allows crew members to transit through U.S. ports. If a person is offered a position, the company provides a letter to support the application. The page says the visa is not required for Canadians or for U.S. citizens or residents. The documentation list asks for the ability to obtain one, with that same exception. Schengen visas are a separate item. The page says some nationalities need one for Europe, and that the hiring partner or HR helps sort out who.

The medical exam is the convention's medical-certificate exception in company language. It is an extensive pre-employment exam at a clinic the company approves. In most cases the employee pays. After the corporate medical team approves it, the paperwork is valid for two years. "In most cases" is the company's phrase. This page will not turn it into "always."

Uniforms are provided on board. Some items count as personal, and crew pay for those. The page says the recruiter or hiring partner will explain which ones. It does not print the list.

If a message asks you, or someone in your family, for money to land an interview or a ship job, the careers page says that is not how hiring works. The hiring partners page points that report to the AWARE line.

## 6. Fourteen hours is one ceiling, and the week has two

The video asks you to guess the legal limit on a single workday. The convention's answer depends on which rule the flag state picked.

Standard A2.3 tells each country to fix one of two regimes. The [2026 FAQ](https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/2026-06/FAQ%20MLC%202006%20Sixth%20edition%202026-EN.pdf) is plain about it. A country sets a maximum on hours of work, or a minimum on hours of rest. Some countries apply both as a matter of their own law. When they do, the FAQ says the shipowner or the master does not get to pick between them.

The work regime: no more than 14 hours in any 24-hour period, and no more than 72 hours in any seven-day period.

The rest regime: at least 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period, and at least 77 hours of rest in any seven-day period.

On a single day the two ceilings meet as arithmetic. Fourteen hours of work leaves ten hours, and ten hours is the daily rest floor. The FAQ says so. Over a week they split. The FAQ says a 72-hour work cap leaves 96 hours of rest in a seven-day period, because 7 times 24 minus 72 is 96. The rest regime's weekly floor is 77 hours. A week has 168 hours. Subtract 77 and 91 hours are left that the rest regime does not require as rest. That 91 is this page's arithmetic from the FAQ's 77-hour floor. The FAQ does not print 91, and the company does not either. Under the work regime the weekly ceiling is 72, which is the stricter week.

A few guardrails apply around either choice, and they keep a clean "14 and done" from being the whole rule.

Hours of rest are time outside hours of work, and the definition leaves out short breaks. The FAQ says a meal break can be a short break when it does not give the seafarer a rest. What counts as short is for the flag's law or the collective agreement. Time away from a station is not automatically rest.

For the rest standard, "any 24-hour period" is a rolling window. The FAQ says a block of 24 hours has to contain the minimum rest no matter where you start the clock.

Rest may be split into two periods at most. One period has to last at least six hours. The gap between rest periods cannot run past 14 hours.

The ship keeps a record of daily hours of work or daily hours of rest. The seafarer receives a copy. The master, or someone the master authorizes, endorses it, and the seafarer endorses it too. A table of working arrangements is posted where people can read it.

Drills are supposed to disturb rest as little as possible, and they are not supposed to cause fatigue. In an emergency, the master can suspend the schedule and require the hours the ship needs. Once the normal situation is back, the seafarer who worked through a scheduled rest gets an adequate period of rest.

A flag state may authorize or register a collective agreement that permits exceptions to these limits. The exceptions are supposed to follow the standard as far as possible. They can take account of more frequent or longer leave, or compensatory leave for watchkeepers and for ships on short voyages. With about 87 percent of this company's shipboard employees under collective agreements, the public ceiling and the contract on a given ship can differ. This page has not seen those agreements.

The same standard also states a normal day. It is based on eight hours, with one day of rest each week and rest on public holidays. A collective agreement can set normal hours, on a basis no less favourable than that. The 14-hour figure is the ceiling in the work regime. The eight-hour figure is the convention's description of a normal day.

Royal Caribbean Group has not published which regime the Bahamas, Cyprus, Malta, or Ecuador applies to its ships, and it has not published the hours recorded on any ship. The careers pages describe contract length. They do not describe a daily roster. The person you see at breakfast and again late in the evening can be inside a legal ceiling and still be tired. Please and thank you cost nothing. A cash tip is your choice. How the automatic daily gratuity is charged is a separate page, [Royal Caribbean Raised Gratuities Again. Where Does the Money Go?](/guides/royal-caribbean-gratuities-where-the-money-goes/).

## 7. The contract is months long, and the ship is assigned

The convention gives seafarers a right to go home. The period before that right arises has to be less than 12 months. The trip is at no cost to the seafarer. The shipowner may not take an advance toward that cost at the start of employment, and may not recover it from wages, except where the seafarer is found, under national law or the applicable agreement, to be in serious default of their employment obligations. Even then, the shipowner pays first. A seafarer can also choose not to use the right when it comes up.

Paid annual leave is a second rule. The minimum is 2.5 calendar days per month of employment. An agreement to give up that minimum leave is prohibited, except in cases the competent authority allows. The ILO's committee reads that exception narrowly, because the point of the leave is rest, health, and a safe ship.

The FAQ reads the leave rule and the repatriation rule together. Its conclusion: the longest stretch on board without leave is, in principle, 11 months. "In principle" is the FAQ's phrase. It is the committee's combined reading, and it lives alongside the exception process and the seafarer's choice about repatriation.

Amendments adopted in 2025 add detail on repatriation costs and on shore leave. The FAQ says those amendments are expected to enter into force on 23 Dec 2027. This page leaves them out of the current list.

The careers FAQ is more specific about this company, and it is still a description of typical contracts, not the union text. Most contracts run four to eight months, depending on the position and the department. Some run shorter. Some run longer. Crew live and work on board for the contract. Accommodation and meals are provided.

Crew do not pick the ship. Assignment follows business needs, a new hire can be sent to any of the group's ships, and a transfer can happen when the company needs it. Couples are welcome, and each person is assessed on their own qualifications. The page says every effort is made to place them on the same ship, and it says the company cannot guarantee that.

The 87 percent figure from the 10-K matters here. Those collective agreements set pay and leave inside the convention's floor. The filing states the percentage. It does not attach the agreements. This page does not have a public copy.

For you, a favorite attendant can finish a contract in the middle of a season, or move to another ship, because the company assigns the ship and the contract has an end. That is the published system. It is a poor reason to take a reassignment personally.

## What to do with this on board

The video sorts the seven rules into two piles. Rules 1 through 4 are about guests and the company: keep it professional, skip the payoff in port, leave the public statement to the people authorized to make it, and report what you see. Rules 5 through 7 are about the crew: no fee for the job, a cap on hours that the flag writes down in one of two ways, and a limit on months at sea before the trip home. The second pile is international law, filled in by national law and by contracts the public does not get.

Three habits follow from the documents.

Keep the friendship at the bar, the pool deck, or wherever that person is working. The guest policy is the one you accepted when you boarded, and it covers Celebrity and Silversea in the copy read for this page, not only the Royal Caribbean brand.

Do not ask a crew member to settle the latest viral story. The code sends that job to Corporate Communications.

If you see something unsafe, or something that may be illegal, tell ship security or Guest Services. The guest policy asks that of you too.
